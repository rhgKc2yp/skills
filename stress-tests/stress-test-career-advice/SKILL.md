# Stress-Test Career Advice

Evaluate career decisions against realistic opportunities, personal constraints, and long-term consequences. Do not confuse popular career narratives with broadly applicable evidence.

## Three-step career audit

1. **Research and assess risk.** Identify career goals, experience, skills, financial constraints, geographic context, timeframe, and available opportunities. Review existing information first; verify relevant labor-market conditions, compensation, qualifications, and industry trends when they could change the recommendation. Assess uncertainty and consequences using judgment. Automatically escalate scrutiny for significant financial exposure, irreversible consequences, serious relationship harm, safety or privacy risks, or major long-term commitments.

2. **Challenge proportionately.** Apply lightweight checks to routine career questions and deeper analysis to consequential choices. Examine survivorship bias, unrealistic expectations, market volatility, transferable skills, opportunity costs, job security, lifestyle effects, and long-term career flexibility. Challenge the credibility, recency, and applicability of external evidence. For consequential questions, explicitly identify the user's most consequential potentially mistaken assumption, if reasonably inferable; explain how it could distort the conclusion without treating it as necessarily false. Never invent a cognitive blind spot. Distinguish individual success stories from reliable expectations. Consider credible counterarguments without manufacturing pessimism.

3. **Evaluate better alternatives.** Explore internal transitions, skill development, negotiation, gradual career changes, informational interviews, or delaying commitments. Compare expected benefits, costs, risks, and flexibility. Recommend alternatives only when reasonably supported. Otherwise, retain the original strategy while identifying ways to reduce uncertainty and downside risk.

## Response

Provide a realistic recommendation, major trade-offs, uncertainties, and practical next steps. For **complex, high-risk career decisions**, include **2–4 decision-relevant counterquestions** with brief findings or explicit unknowns. Cite relevant sources when available; avoid unsupported predictions or promises.
